Kitchen Faucet Handle Repair: Minnetonka, MN Homeowner Guide

To repair a kitchen faucet handle, find out whether it is loose, stiff or leaking, then tighten the set screw or replace the cartridge. Most Minnetonka kitchens need one tool and an hour.

By the Minnetonka 24/7 Plumbing team. Published 2026-10-22. Updated 2026-10-22.

Call (952) 866-9120

A loose handle is usually a screw, not a dead faucet

A kitchen faucet handle is a lever that turns a valve inside the faucet body. When the handle wobbles, the cause is almost always a small set screw that backed out. When it feels gritty or stiff, the culprit is the cartridge or the stem inside, coated with mineral scale. When water seeps around its base, an O-ring or seal has worn out.

None of these means you need a new faucet. Each is a part that costs a fraction of a replacement, and most are reachable with a hex key and a flat screwdriver. The trick is naming the symptom first, so you buy the right part once.

Faucet work is covered by the Minnesota Plumbing Code, Minnesota Rules chapter 4714, and the Wayzata city page says like-for-like faucet swaps there do not need a permit if the piping is unchanged, which is a local standard and not a state one.

Replacing a single-handle cartridge

This is the repair behind most stiff and dripping handles. Allow an hour and a towel for the sink bowl.

  1. Shut off the water and relieve pressure

    Close both stop valves under the sink by turning them clockwise. Open the faucet to drain what is left in the lines. If a stop valve will not turn or keeps leaking after you close it, stop there, because the next move is a plumber, not a harder twist.

  2. Remove the handle

    Pry off the small cap on the side or top, then loosen the set screw it hides with a hex key. Lift the handle off. If it is stuck, a rag soaked in warm vinegar around its base for ten minutes often loosens the scale.

  3. Pull the old cartridge

    Unscrew the retaining nut or clip, then twist and lift out the cartridge. Photograph its orientation before it leaves the faucet, because it goes back in only one way.

  4. Match and install the new one

    Take the old cartridge to a plumbing supply counter so the new one matches in brand and size. Coat the new O-rings with plumber's grease, seat the cartridge, tighten the nut, replace the handle, then open the stops slowly and check for drips.

Do not skip the little stuff

Clean the inside of the faucet body with a soft brush before the new cartridge goes in. Grit left behind scores the new O-rings and you will be back under the sink in a month. Run the faucet at full flow for a minute with the aerator off to flush any debris out of the spout.

Three leaks that look like handle problems

Water under the sink is not always the handle's fault. Check these before you buy parts.

Supply line connections

Dry a braided supply line with a paper towel, then run your fingers along it. A damp nut at the stop valve or faucet shank points to a loose or worn connection, not the cartridge.

A cracked sprayer hose

On pull-down faucets, the hose under the sink can wear against the cabinet and drip only when the sprayer is in use.

Condensation on cold lines

In humid weather, cold supply lines sweat and the drips look like a leak. Wipe them dry and watch whether the wetness returns after the weather changes.

Why it wears out faster in some kitchens

Cartridges are ceramic or brass discs sliding against rubber seals. Hard water leaves calcium and magnesium on those surfaces, which grinds the seals and sticks the handle. A kitchen with heavy scale on the aerator and showerhead usually has the same buildup inside the faucet.

If the same faucet fails every couple of years, the answer may not be another cartridge. A water softener or filtration system treats the water for the whole house, and it also protects the water heater and dishwasher.

What Minnetonka homeowners should know about hardness and faucet parts

Your water decides how often you do this repair. Hopkins states on its city water page that its water is 21 grains of hardness, which is high and means cartridges there scale up quickly. Eden Prairie treats its water at its own plant, and the city says treated tap water averages about 5 grains per gallon.

The City of Minnetonka's Drinking Water Report does not print a hardness figure, so test your tap with a hardness strip kit or ask your water utility. The Minnesota Department of Health says a softener may be worth considering when hardness runs above 7 grains per gallon. Softener brine also ends up as waste, so size and settings matter.

Source: City of Hopkins and City of Eden Prairie water pages, Minnesota Department of Health.

Before you drive to the supply counter

  • Take a phone photo of the faucet brand mark and the handle style.
  • Bring the old cartridge or stem in a sandwich bag.
  • Buy new O-rings and a small tube of plumber's grease.
  • Check that your hex key size fits the set screw before you start.
  • Put a towel or dish rag over the drain so small parts cannot fall in.
  • Skip thread tape on the cartridge, since most seal with O-rings.

Repair, replace or hand it off

Repair when the faucet body is sound, the finish is intact and parts are available. Replace when the body is pitted, the handle housing is cracked, or the faucet is old enough that makers no longer stock cartridges. A faucet repair visit from a licensed plumber covers both and gets the stop valves checked at the same time.

For Hopkins kitchens with a long history of scale, Hopkins plumbers can compare the faucet, stop valves and supply lines in one stop. If you live south of Minnetonka, a plumber serving Eden Prairie can swap the faucet and test the shutoffs the same day when appointments are available.
