Sump Pump Installation Cost in Minnetonka, MN: What Drives It

Sump pump installation cost in Minnetonka depends less on the pump than on the pit, the discharge line, the power and the permit. Here are the factors, a low-to-high table, and the questions to ask.

By the Minnetonka 24/7 Plumbing team. Published 2026-10-22. Updated 2026-10-22.

Call (952) 866-9120

What you are really paying for

A sump pump moves groundwater out of a pit in the basement floor and discharges it outdoors, away from the foundation. The pump itself is the smallest part of the job. Most of the labor is the pit, the plumbing that carries water outside, and the electrical connection.

Two houses can get the same pump and very different bills. A basement that already has a pit and a short run to the exterior wall is a straightforward swap. A basement with solid slab, no pit and a long run to daylight is a bigger project, and the quote should show that difference line by line.

Installation is plumbing work covered by Minnesota Rules chapter 4714, and the Wayzata page says sump pump replacement needs no permit if the piping is not altered, so the standards differ by city.

Cost factors on a sump pump installation

No two quotes line up unless these five factors match. Ask what each quote assumes.

New pit or existing pitCutting the slab, digging a pit, setting a liner and patching concrete adds labor and mess. Reusing a sound pit is much cheaper.
Pump type and sizeA submersible pump sits in the pit and runs quietly. A pedestal pump costs less but is louder and less forgiving. Horsepower and switch type change the price.
Discharge line length and routeThe farther the water must travel to reach daylight, the more pipe, fittings and digging. A check valve and a frost-safe outlet add parts.
Power and backupA dedicated GFCI outlet and a battery backup or water-powered backup add parts and electrical time, but protect you in an outage.
Permit and inspectionCities charge based on the project's valuation. A permit may also trigger extra inspection fees, so ask what the quote includes.

Many policies cover sudden burst-pipe damage but exclude water that seeps in from the ground or backs up from a drain unless you added a rider. Read your policy and call your insurer before you decide whether a pump or backup is worth the cost.

Submersible or pedestal: the pump choice

A submersible pump sits in the water, runs quietly and handles a finished basement well. A pedestal pump keeps its motor above the pit, so it is easier to reach, but it is noisy and wears faster. Cast iron housings last longer than plastic ones.

Float switches fail more often than motors. A vertical float or an electronic switch costs more up front and sticks less in a narrow pit. Ask which switch comes in the quote.

Questions to ask before you sign

  • Is the pit existing, or will the slab be cut? Who patches the concrete?
  • Where will the discharge outlet end, and how will it avoid freezing and flowing back?
  • Does the price include a check valve, a GFCI outlet and the permit?
  • What is the backup if the power fails or the primary pump dies?
  • Is the discharge line going outdoors and not to a sanitary drain?
  • Who handles cleanup and testing, and is there a written warranty on parts and labor?

Water and electricity do not mix

Pump power must run through a GFCI-protected outlet, and never through a plugged-together chain of extension cords near the pit. If the pit is full and the pump is not running, do not reach in with your hand. Unplug it at the outlet only from a dry spot, or have a plumber do it. A battery backup, or a water-powered backup tied to the water line, keeps pumping if the grid goes dark in a storm.

Why the discharge line matters more than people expect

An outdoor discharge that ends next to the foundation sends water straight back toward the pit, so the pump cycles endlessly. It should end well away from the wall, on ground that slopes away from the house. In winter, a short open outlet can freeze solid and force water to back up into the pit.

Never tie the discharge into a sanitary sewer drain. Rules on this vary by city, so ask yours before you plumb it. Sump discharge goes outdoors or to a storm connection the city allows. A sump pump repair visit checks the whole path from pit to outlet.

What Hopkins and Wayzata homeowners should know about sump pump permits and runoff

The City of Hopkins says its stormwater drains to the Minnehaha Creek and Nine Mile Creek watersheds. That means your discharge line should flow onto your lot and away from neighbors, not into a sanitary drain, and you should ask the city where else it is allowed to go.

Wayzata's city page says it will not require permits, testing and inspection for plumbing fixture replacements such as replacing a sump pump, as long as the work does not alter the plumbing piping systems. A new pit or a rerouted discharge is a bigger job, so ask Metro West Inspections. Minnetonka's form, covered next, lists no exemptions.

Permits in Minnetonka itself

The City of Minnetonka's plumbing permit application says work is not to start without a permit. Residential permits start at $77 for valuations of $1 to $1,000, fees are based on project valuation, and other fees such as plan review or re-inspection may apply. The form states no exemptions, so ask the City of Minnetonka building department whether your project needs one.

Ask whether the permit is part of your quote and who pulls it. If you are adding drain tile or a perimeter system, see how it fits with basement waterproofing before you buy a pump alone.

Hopkins and Wayzata: where to start

Hopkins plumbers work with homes where about 60 percent of owner-occupied units are at least 60 years old, which means many basements have old slabs and original drain lines to check before a new pit goes in. In Wayzata, the lakeside setting is a reason to ask about a battery backup, since groundwater can rise in wet seasons.
