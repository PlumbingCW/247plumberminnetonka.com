Sewer Camera Inspection in Minnetonka, MN

A sewer camera inspection in Minnetonka sends a waterproof camera through your drain or sewer line and records what is inside, so repairs and purchases rest on video rather than guesses.

What the camera sees inside your line

A drain camera is a small, sealed lens on a flexible rod, with lights around it. It enters through a cleanout, a pulled toilet or a roof vent, and a monitor shows the pipe in real time. A transmitter in the head lets a locator on the surface follow its path and measure its depth.

The picture shows pipe material, joints, the water level, scale on iron, roots, cracks, offsets and bellies. It also shows whether a stoppage was a one-time plug or a sign that something in the pipe has changed. We record the run, so you can watch it and keep a copy.

The line is usually cleaned first so the lens can see. A pipe full of grease or paper looks like a solid wall on screen. A camera inspection finds the problem, and any repair that follows is governed by the Minnesota Plumbing Code in Minnesota Rules chapter 4714, plus the standards of the city that issues your permit.

What the camera finds, what it means and what comes next

Not every finding needs a dig. This table shows common results and typical next steps.

Before you buy a house: the pre-purchase look

A general home inspection looks at fixtures and visible plumbing, but it cannot see an underground sewer lateral. A camera inspection fills the gap. For a buyer, it answers whether the line is sound, what material it is and whether roots or breaks are already there.

This matters most for older homes. Much of Minnetonka's single-family housing was built between 1950 and 1970, so a house of that era may have original clay, cast iron or fiber pipe still serving it. If the video shows trouble, you can ask the seller for repairs, adjust the offer or at least budget for the work.

Schedule the camera during your inspection period so there is time to act on what it shows. Ask the seller for cleanout access, and tell us if the house is vacant or on well and septic, since that changes the plan.

Recurring clogs are a reason to look, not to clear again

A line that clogs once is usually a fluke. A line that clogs every few months is trying to tell you something. Each extra cabling visit costs money and rarely fixes the cause, which might be a sag, a root intrusion or a collapsed section.

If your drain has been cleared and slowed again, put a camera on it. Then the next step, whether it is hydro jetting, a repair or a maintenance plan, is based on the pipe, not on repeating what did not work. Our drain cleaning page describes the usual first-line options.

Finding where the line runs

Sometimes the question is not what is wrong, but where the pipe goes. Before a landscaping job, a deck or an addition, you need to know where the lateral runs and how deep it is. We send the camera head in, and a locator receiver above ground picks up its signal, so we can mark the path on the surface.

Marking a private line does not replace a utility locate for public lines. Before any digging in the yard, Minnesota homeowners should arrange public utility locates through the state's call-before-you-dig system.

When waiting is a mistake

Camera work is not an emergency in itself, but some signs should not wait. A sewer smell that keeps returning, a sunken patch in the yard, or a drain that backs up when the washer runs all point to trouble. If sewage reaches the house, go to our sewer line cleaning page, or call the 24/7 line at once.

How an inspection visit goes

The visit is short and the steps are simple.

  1. Tell us what you see

    Share which fixtures are slow, and whether the home is being bought or sold, so we bring the right camera and locator.

  2. Access and clean

    We find the cleanout. If the pipe is dirty, we clear it enough to see the walls.

  3. Run the camera

    The camera travels to the connection, recording the pipe while we note distances and depth.

  4. Review the video together

    We play it back, explain every defect and give you a plain recommendation. A repair quote, if needed, is given before any work starts.

What affects the cost of a camera inspection

Camera work is a modest cost compared to a blind dig. These factors change the price.

Line length and accessA short run from a clear cleanout is quick. A long lateral, or one with no cleanout, takes longer.
Cleaning before the cameraA heavily clogged pipe needs cleaning first, which adds to the visit.
Locating and markingTracing the line with a locator and marking the depth is added work.
Written report and video copyA recorded copy helps with buyers, sellers and insurers. Ask for one when you book.
SchedulingA planned weekday visit is simpler than an urgent call. Same-day appointments are available when the schedule allows.

Insurers treat an inspection as a maintenance or purchase cost, not a covered loss. If the camera finds damage from a sudden event, bring the video to your insurer, and read your policy to see what applies.
