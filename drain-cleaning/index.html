Emergency Drain Cleaning in Minnetonka, MN

Drain cleaning in Minnetonka means clearing a blocked or slow pipe with a cable, a hydro jetter or a camera-guided plan so water leaves your home again. Call the 24/7 line and we will work out which fixture, which pipe and which tool.

Which fixtures are slow tells us where the clog sits

Before anyone touches a tool, the pattern of symptoms narrows the search. A single slow sink points at that sink's own trap or branch line. Several fixtures slow at once points downstream, toward the main drain. Use the table below to match what you see to the likely problem.

What you noticeLikely locationUsual method
One fixture slow or stopped.Trap or branch line at that fixture.Hand tools, small cable.
Every fixture slow.Main house drain or the line to the street.Machine cable, then camera.
Gurgling after a flush.Partial blockage or venting trouble.Cable, then vent check.
Sewer smell near a floor drain.Dry trap or a partly blocked branch.Refill trap, cable if needed.
Water backing into the tub.Main drain blocked, lowest fixture shows it first.Machine cable or hydro jetter.

Water showing up in a basement tub or floor drain while upstairs sinks still empty is the classic sign of a blocked main. Treat that one as urgent, and read the emergency section further down.

What is usually inside a clogged drain

A clogged drain is almost never one big object. It is a slow build of grease, hair, soap film and food scraps that narrows the pipe until the next handful of paper tips it into a full stop. Bathroom clogs lean toward hair and soap. Kitchen clogs lean toward fat and food. Laundry lines collect lint and detergent residue.

Pouring a store-bought chemical opener into a stopped pipe tends to make the job harder. The liquid sits on top of the blockage, eats at the pipe wall and can splash a technician who later opens the trap. If the water has not moved after one try, stop and call.

To unclog a drain properly, a plumber removes the blockage rather than thinning it. That usually means a cable for soft clogs, a hydro jetter for coatings and roots, or a drain camera when the same line keeps stopping. Drain and waste piping falls under the Minnesota Plumbing Code in Minnesota Rules chapter 4714, and the standards for licensing come from the Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry.

How drain cabling works, and when a cable is not enough

Drain snaking, also called cabling, feeds a coiled steel cable into the pipe while a motor spins it. The cutting head bores through the blockage or hooks it so it can be pulled back out. For hair, paper and most kitchen clogs, a cable is quick and does the job.

A cable has limits. It punches a hole through a clog, and in a greasy line the hole can close again within weeks because the grease on the pipe wall is still there. It also struggles with thick root mats and cannot scrub the pipe clean. When a line clears and then slows again, the cable did its job and the pipe still needs more.

That is the point to step up to hydro jetting, which sends high-pressure water through a nozzle that washes the full circumference of the pipe. If the same line has stopped more than once, we often run a camera first so the next step is based on what is actually in there.

Kitchen sink clogs: grease, food, the disposal and the dishwasher line

Kitchen drains are the busiest and the greasiest in the house. Cooking fat goes down warm and liquid, then cools into a soft white wall on the pipe interior. Coffee grounds, rice and pasta stick to it, and the passage shrinks a little more each week.

A garbage disposal grinds food into small pieces, but it does not make grease disappear. Those pieces settle in the first horizontal run of pipe past the trap. The dishwasher drain hose also ties into the disposal or the sink tailpiece, so a clog there can send gray water into the dishwasher tub or back up through the second basin of a double sink.

For a kitchen stoppage we check the trap and the disposal outlet first, then cable the branch line toward the wall. If the blockage sits farther in, we cable or jet toward the main. A jammed or humming disposal is its own repair, and our garbage disposal repair page covers it.

Minnetonka homes, older pipes and the question of who owns the line

Much of Minnetonka's single-family housing was built between 1950 and 1970, and about 56% of the city's homes are single-family detached. Homes from that era often still have their original drain lines, so repeated clogs in a house like that justify a camera look rather than a fifth round of cabling.

The other big group is apartment and condo living. Roughly 29% of Minnetonka's housing units sit in buildings of 20 or more units, where several apartments share a vertical drain stack. A clog in a shared stack shows up in the lowest unit first, even though the blockage may be floors above.

On the ownership question: the city runs its own utilities through Minnetonka Public Works, but the pipe from your house to the connection point is usually your responsibility. If you are unsure where your property's side ends, ask the city utilities office before you assume the main is the city's problem. Our plumber can tell you what the camera shows, and you can take that to the city.

Urgent

When a backed-up drain is an emergency

A drain backing up into a tub, a shower or a basement floor drain is an emergency, and it does not wait for business hours. Our 24/7 line takes these calls at night and on weekends, and same-day appointments are available when the schedule allows.

Wastewater in the house is not clean water, so while you wait, reduce what goes in and keep people and pets out of it.

  1. Stop running water in the house. No showers, no laundry, no dishwasher cycles.
  2. Do not flush. Every flush sends more water into the blockage.
  3. Keep children and pets away from standing wastewater.
  4. Move belongings off the floor if the water is still rising.
  5. Skip the chemical opener. It can burn a technician and does not clear a main line.
  6. Note which fixtures are slow or backing up and tell the dispatcher. It tells us where the blockage sits.

When the clog is really a leaking drain line

Occasionally a drain that keeps clogging is hiding a leak. A cracked or separated joint lets water and waste seep into the soil or slab, and roots follow that moisture into the opening. The warning signs are a damp spot under a basement slab crack, a musty smell with no visible source, or a line that clears and refills within days.

Cabling cannot fix a broken pipe. When we suspect a leak, we run a drain camera and, if needed, hand the job to a repair plan. The sewer camera inspection page explains what the camera can show.

What a drain call looks like

Every call goes a little differently, but the sequence is steady.

  1. Dispatch asks the right questions

    Which fixtures are affected, how long it has been going on, and whether water is rising. This tells us which tools to bring.

  2. The plumber confirms the location

    We test fixtures, check the cleanout if there is one, and decide whether the problem is a branch line or the main.

  3. You hear the price before work starts

    We explain the method and the cost, and you decide. If the first method will not hold, we say so up front.

  4. We clear it and prove it

    After cabling or jetting, we run water through the line to confirm flow, and tell you what we pulled out.

Habits that keep drains open between visits

A few small habits reduce how often you need us.

  • Wipe greasy pans with a paper towel and bin it instead of rinsing fat down the sink.
  • Fit a mesh strainer on the tub and shower drains to catch hair.
  • Flush toilet paper only. Wipes labelled flushable still tangle in older pipes.
  • Run hot water for a minute after the dishwasher finishes.
  • Know where your main cleanout is, so a plumber can reach the line fast.
  • Treat a drain that slows twice in a month as a signal to call, not a coincidence.

Check any plumber's licence before the work starts

Minnesota requires a plumbing contractor licence for plumbing work in communities over 5,000 people. You can look up any plumber or company on the Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry Check a License page. Our plumbers are licensed and insured, and you can compare notes with that lookup.
